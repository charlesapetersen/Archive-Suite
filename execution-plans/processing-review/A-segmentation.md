## Question

Our segmenter has to decide, for a box photographed page by page on a phone, where each document starts, with box and folder label photographs interleaved, under the owner's document rules (an enclosure with its own date is its own document; an attachment is not; a clipping is one document per article; a full magazine is one document; a repeat photograph is a continuation). It is scored by the share of pages that land in an exactly right document. On the locked test boxes the best cloud window method puts 87-92% of pages right unchecked and reaches 97-100% after a review of 10-24 boundaries per 100 pages; the trained Qwen3.5 2B on the Mac puts 78.8% right and its confidence does not rank its errors. On the Lincoln Papers, a 19th-century manuscript box, the Qwen family falls to 35-47%. The question for this strand is what the field's methods, data and scorers add to that picture: whether any public benchmark or model should replace or anchor our own boxes, what explains and what would fix the Lincoln drop, how a new collection can be adapted to with few labels or from the user's corrections, and whether a user-trained model is a sensible kit for this stage.

## What the field does now

The task is called page stream segmentation (PSS): given an ordered stream of pages, label each page as the start of a document or a continuation. Almost every method decides each boundary from the adjacent pair, or from a short window, rather than from one page alone. Single-page classification is measurably worse: in the Multi-Split paper (A04) zero-shot cloud models score boundary F1 0.67-0.75 when shown one page and 0.84-0.88 when shown the adjacent pair on TABME++ (Table 3). Our own history is the same shape: the spring per-page prompt, tuned on all five boxes, reached 86.5% of pages in an exact document; an untuned 7-page window with Claude reached 90.3% (SUMMARY.md).

Three families of method are current.

First, fine-tuned small decoders on OCR text. Heidenreich et al. (A05) LoRA-tune Mistral-7B and Phi-3-mini on the text of the previous and current page and reach page F1 0.987 and document F1 0.967 on TABME++, against 0.56-0.64 document F1 for every encoder they tried (RoBERTa, LayoutLMv3, LiLT, DiT) and 0.642 for XGBoost on TF-IDF (Table 4). Zero-shot GPT-4o on the same data scores 0.703 document F1. Training converges fast (page F1 0.977 after 5,000 updates of batch 16, Figure 2) and stopped at 0.3 of an epoch. This is the result our students reproduce: untrained Qwen3.5 4B agreed with the cleaned labels on 77% of held-out gaps, fine-tuned 97.6%, and the pilot on a quarter of the windows already 97.1% (seg-students.md).

Second, small supervised image-plus-text models with sequence smoothing, shipped as products. Nutrient's doc-split-v1 (A20) is an open ONNX model: a 512x512 image tower, a 512-token text tower gated off when a page has no text, a boundary head, a two-state CRF over the stream, and a beta calibration of the confidence. It scores boundary F1 0.859 on OpenPSS-long, 0.585 on OpenPSS-short, 0.936 on its own synthetic set, and zero-shot 0.704 on TABME++ and 0.820 on Tobacco800 (card, results table). The academic equivalents are the OpenPSS paper's BERT-plus-EfficientNet late ensemble (page F1 0.83 LONG, 0.76 SHORT; Table 2) and the TABME multimodal LayoutLM-plus-ResNet model (page F1 0.953 on TABME, quoted in A05 Table 5).

Third, zero-shot prompting of cloud vision-language models over pairs or windows. A04 measures it systematically: Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite with text input scores 0.883 on adjacent pairs on TABME++ and 0.870, 0.863, 0.861 at windows of 5, 7 and 10 pages; Claude Haiku 4.5 falls faster (0.866 at pairs, 0.782 at 7, 0.726 at 10), GPT-5.4-mini likewise (0.873, 0.739, 0.693) (Table 4). Vision input gives no consistent gain over text once the adjacent page is in view, and degrades faster with window size (Section 6, Appendix A). The paper recommends choosing the window size on a small labelled validation set (Section 8) and gives no number of labels. DocSplit (A01) frames the richer "packet" task, where pages may be shuffled or interleaved and must be grouped, typed and ordered; its best model (Qwen3-VL 235B, text from Textract) groups pages at a clustering score of 0.86-0.90 and orders them at 0.98-1.00, so boundary grouping is the bottleneck (Table 2, Section 8).

On evaluation, the field has moved from page-level F1 to document-level scores because page-level scores hide the damage of one wrong cut. OpenPSS adopts Panoptic Quality from computer vision: a predicted document matches a true one when their page sets overlap at IoU above 0.5, recognition quality (RQ) is the F1 of those matches, segmentation quality (SQ) their mean IoU, and PQ = RQ x SQ (Section 3.5). Heidenreich et al. use document F1 on exact page sets, a straight-through-processing rate (share of streams segmented perfectly) and the minimum number of drag-and-drops a person would need to fix the result (Section 4.3). Nutrient's scorer adds Cohen's kappa, WindowDiff and Pk, with kappa there because a predict-all or predict-none model can look fine on F1 at a skewed boundary rate (A22 card). DocSplit adds clustering scores (V-measure, Rand), Kendall tau for order, and page accuracy at three strictness levels (A01 Section 5).

On robustness, the one published cross-collection measurement is OpenPSS's "robust" task: models trained on LONG and tested on SHORT and the reverse lose 23% to 47% of their weighted document F1, text models least (TEXT-CNN 23%, BERT 33%), image models most (VGG16 and EfficientNet 46%), and the ensembles in between (Figure 3, Table 3). Nutrient's open model shows the same thing between corpora (0.936 in-domain, 0.704 and 0.820 zero-shot elsewhere). On adaptation with few labels, learning from a reviewer's corrections, or active learning for PSS, the screen found nothing and two further searches found nothing. The nearest items are the recommendation to pick a window size on a small validation set (A04), configuration search against a small labelled set (A25, not read), and the finding in the zbMATH work (A26, Section 8.4) that neither OCR confidence nor the model's token probabilities predicted its errors.

## Comparison with what we measured

What transfers.

1. The formulation. Pair or window decisions with context are the field's standard, and the gain over single-page classification is the field's most consistent result (A04 Table 3; A05 Table 4 encoders versus pairs). Our move from the spring per-page prompt to windows follows it, and the sizes agree in direction.

2. Fine-tuned small decoders beat encoders and zero-shot models, and most of the gain arrives early. A05's 0.967 document F1 against 0.56-0.64 for encoders, and its convergence in the first few thousand updates, match our students (77% to 97.6% agreement on held-out gaps; the quarter-data pilot already at 97.1%).

3. Fine-tuned models lose badly across collections, and image features lose most. OpenPSS's robust task (23-47% relative drop, image models worst) and doc-split-v1's zero-shot drop (0.936 to 0.704 and 0.820) are the same phenomenon as our students' 97.6% on held-out collections of the same owner, 79% on his two unseen boxes, and 35-47% on Lincoln. Our students were trained with 29% of windows carrying no text, and the feature model found the image feature print its third most important feature; both lean on image evidence, which is the brittle kind.

4. Confidence from a fine-tuned model does not rank its errors. A26 Section 8.4 found neither OCR confidence nor token probabilities predicted the errors of a 96%-accurate fine-tuned 7B; our students need about 80 checks per 100 pages to reach 98%, close to checking every gap. The cloud models' confidence works better for us (Claude 10.2 checks per 100 pages), and two-model disagreement is a better flag still, but the models err alike on the hard gaps (Dean: Claude and Gemini took the same decision on every gap; ensemble-test.md).

5. Window size interacts with the model. A04 finds Gemini flat to K=10 and Claude Haiku and GPT-5.4-mini falling from K=3. Our whole-folder Gemini 3.8 Flash call (93.1% on dev) beat the same model on 10-page pieces (89.4%), so a strong model can use more context than A04's weaker ones could. The lesson that transfers is the procedure (sweep K on a small labelled set per corpus), not any particular K.

6. Document-level metrics. Our headline (pages in an exact document) is in the family the field now recommends. Heidenreich's exact-tuple document F1 is our "docs exact" expressed as precision and recall; MNDD and STP are the two published correction-effort measures and are cheap to add; PQ gives partial credit at IoU above 0.5 and kappa guards against degenerate predictors. Our development boxes have a boundary rate of about 59% (292 true boundaries among 494 document gaps, from the all-new baseline's 202 false splits), Lincoln 31% (80 of 260), CREST 24% (30 of 127), so our "every page a document" baseline scores 39% of pages and boundary F1 0.743 on dev, which is exactly the case kappa is for.

What does not transfer.

1. The data. Every public set is a scan: TABME++ and Tobacco800 at 300 DPI from microfilm or paper, OpenPSS from government PDFs with redactions, DocSplit from RVL-CDIP scans, our-200 from synthetic concatenation. None is photographed on a phone, none has show-through, a telegram lying on a full sheet, paper-colour changes, or box and folder label photographs in the stream. None has 19th-century handwriting. TABME++ is the closest in genre (20th-century US typed business papers) and the farthest in capture. The owner's domain cues (02-truth-check.md) have no counterpart in any public set.

2. Synthetic streams of complete documents. TABME++, DocSplit and our-200 concatenate whole documents of at most 20 pages drawn from an archive, with 55% single-page documents in TABME++ and a 78% boundary rate in our-200. A real box has long runs (Lincoln's 22-canvas Corning manuscript), enclosures inside letters, and clippings whose first page starts mid-article. The published boundary F1 figures therefore sit higher than a box would give the same model.

3. The cloud-VLM numbers on OpenPSS-long. The leaderboard's 0.244 for gemini-flash, 0.025 for gpt-sol and 0.047 for claude-opus come from whole-stream prompting against per-request image caps (about 100, 200 and 500 images); streams over the cap are scored as predict-none, and the page says that drives the long-stream results. OpenPSS-long streams have a median of 217 pages. These figures say nothing about windowed prompting and should not be read as the warning the screen took them for. On the short streams (median 60 pages) gemini-flash scored 0.598 against doc-split-v1's 0.585, and on our-200 gpt-sol 0.942 against 0.936.

4. Text-only sufficiency. A04's finding that vision adds nothing once the adjacent page's text is in view rests on Microsoft OCR of clean scans. Our material has a third of its cues in the image (show-through to be ignored, a small item on a full sheet, paper colour, handwriting), and our Apple Vision text on photographs is weaker than their OCR. Whether text-only windows hold up on our boxes is a test, not a result.

5. Document length and the metric. Under our metric one false split inside a 22-page manuscript takes 22 pages out of "exact"; under PQ that document would still match at IoU above 0.5 and score SQ 0.9. Lincoln's 40 false splits cost the teacher 60% of pages while finding every true boundary. Part of the Lincoln figure is therefore the metric's severity on long documents, which is the right severity for a user whose file is wrongly cut, but it means Lincoln is not comparable with any published figure.

Does a public benchmark make our own boxes less necessary? No. None has photographs, handwriting, clippings, label photos or the owner's rules, and the bench's locked boxes remain the primary test. Two public sets are worth running as anchors, for two different reasons: TABME++ because its genre is ours and it has published figures for fine-tuned decoders (0.987 page F1), encoders (0.78-0.83) and zero-shot cloud models at several window sizes (0.74-0.88), so it calibrates our students and windows against the field; OpenPSS because it is real, heterogeneous and the only set with a cross-collection protocol, and it has a published open model (doc-split-v1, 0.859) and a specialist (0.83 page F1) to compare with. DocSplit's data are synthetic, text-only and non-commercial; only its clustering and ordering scores are worth borrowing, and only if we start seeing non-contiguous documents (misfiled or interleaved pages), which our boxes rarely have.

Scorers to borrow and run ourselves: Nutrient's `score.py` (kappa, WindowDiff, Pk, boundary F1 with page 0 forced); PQ, RQ and SQ as OpenPSS defines them; document F1 on exact page sets, STP and MNDD as Heidenreich defines them. All are cheap to add to `run.py` and to apply to every cached answer. Our bench already reports Pk and WindowDiff.

## The generalisation problem, adaptation, corrections and the user-trained kit

What the Lincoln figures actually show. The 35-47% is the Qwen family only (the 27B zero-shot teacher at 39.5%, the LoRA students at 34.9% and 46.7%); no cloud model has run on Lincoln. The counts in `lincoln-scores.json` show the failure mode: the teacher found 80 of 80 true boundaries and added 40 false ones; the 4B found 78 and added 32; the 2B found 78 and added 44. Boundary recall is 0.97-1.00 and precision 0.64-0.71, so the models over-split long handwritten documents. CREST, which the same models segment at 91-98%, is typed 20th-century government paper of the kind the owner's prompt cues and the students' training windows describe. The field offers three readings and the counts favour the first two: (a) distribution shift in the visual and textual cues (OpenPSS: image models lose most; the prompt's cues are salutations, memo headings, bylines and page numbers, which a 19th-century manuscript letter does not carry, so the model falls back on "text ending mid-page" and "a new hand", which fire inside a long manuscript); (b) a boundary prior learned from the owner's corpus, where 59% of documents are one page, applied to a box where the mean document is 3.2 pages and the longest 22; (c) the sparse-boundary regime, which doc-split-v1's card calls the hardest, though CREST is sparser (24%) than Lincoln (31%) and was easy, so sparsity alone does not explain it. The window-size reading (A04) does not fit: a 7-page window is not what makes a model cut a manuscript in half. The first test below settles whether the cloud windows share the failure; if Gemini and Claude hold near 90% on Lincoln, the drop is the open models' weakness on handwriting and the fix is routing, not retraining; if they also fall, the material needs a continuity cue (same hand, catchwords, running text across the page break) that no current prompt or model carries.

Adaptation with few labels. No paper measures a learning curve for a new collection; the field's advice stops at "a small labelled validation set" (A04) for a hyperparameter and 1,000 hand-labelled samples (A26) for a LoRA. Our own evidence is that a quarter of 5,885 teacher-labelled windows reached 97.1% in-domain, which is still about 1,500 windows. What can be refit from tens of labels: a decision threshold, a boundary-rate prior, doc-split-v1's CRF transition weights and its beta calibration, and the window size. What needs hundreds to thousands: a LoRA pass. The experiment that answers the question is a learning curve on Lincoln and on Stanton, at 0, 25, 50, 100 and 200 labelled gaps, for each of: threshold and prior refit; CRF and calibration refit on doc-split-v1; few-shot examples placed in the cloud prompt; a LoRA continuation of the 2B. It is free on FarmShare or Sherlock for the open models and under $3 for Gemini on these two boxes. The result decides what the review screen's corrections can buy.

Learning from user corrections. The Processor's flagged-boundary review already produces exactly the labels: a keep, split or join decision per gap with the page images and text beside it. The field's contribution here is only the measure of effort (MNDD, TABME's drag-and-drops, STP), which our "boundaries to check per 100 pages" and "edits per 100 pages" already cover, and the warning that a fine-tuned model's own confidence will not choose the right gaps to show (A26; our students). The design that follows is: store corrections per collection; after each box refit the cheap layer (threshold, prior, calibration) on the Mac; when enough corrections exist, offer a LoRA pass to profile 4 and 5 users; measure each refit on the held-out remainder of the same collection before trusting it. Two-model disagreement, not one model's confidence, chooses the gaps to show. There is no published PSS active-learning result to lean on, so this is ours to measure.

A user-trained model as a kit. The evidence for it: the recipe is published (A05 Appendix B) and ours works (`train_seg.py`, one L40S, one epoch, 5,885 windows, 97.6% on held-out collections of the same owner); the trained 2B runs on a 16 GB Mac at 18 s a window with 4.4 GB peak; a user with a corpus already tagged per document, as the owner's HBS and Doriot folders were, has the labels without labelling. The evidence against: the same students fell to 79% on the owner's own unseen boxes and to 35-47% on Lincoln; the teacher that labelled them is itself at 39.5% on Lincoln, so distillation inherits the teacher's blind spot, and on handwriting the labels must come from a person; the field's one cross-collection measurement says image-leaning models lose about half their document-level score on a new corpus. The verdict is that the kit is sensible for the stage only in a narrow form: for a user with a tagged corpus or several hundred reviewed gaps in the same collection, as a cheap private voter beside the cloud or as the only route for a laptop-only user, and never without the bench, so that the user sees the held-out score on their own box before the model segments anything. It should not be presented as a replacement for the cloud windows, and its first job on a new collection should be to flag disagreement with the cloud voter rather than to decide alone.

## Tests to run on our material, ranked

Each with the profile it serves, its free route and whether money is needed. Prices from `options.md`: Gemini 3.8 Flash $3.61 per 1,000 pages at list, $1.81 batch; Claude Sonnet 5.5 $16.93 (or the Claude plan, no money); Codex plan no money.

1. Cloud windows on Lincoln and CREST (389 pages). Gemini 3.8 Flash through the existing window method: about $1.40 at list, under $1 in batch. Claude through the plan route and ChatGPT through Codex: no money. Settles whether the Lincoln drop is the open models' or the material's, and gives the first cloud figure on a public box. Serves profiles 2-3; also decides what profile 1 and 5 can be promised. Paid: yes, under $3. Note the paid-hosted hold of 2026-10-17: this is one of the deferred runs in `paid-runs-deferred.md` and needs the owner to release it, or the plan routes alone.

2. Add the field's scorers to the bench and re-score every cached answer: kappa, WindowDiff and Pk from `score.py`; PQ, RQ and SQ; document F1, STP and MNDD. Free, Mac, an afternoon. Shows how much of Lincoln is the metric's severity on long documents (PQ will match the over-split manuscripts; our metric rightly will not), and gives the owner the correction-effort number the field publishes. All profiles.

3. doc-split-v1 on the five owner boxes, Lincoln and CREST, zero-shot, then with its CRF transitions and beta calibration refit on the three development boxes. Free, Mac CPU, onnxruntime; our Apple Vision text supplies the text tower. Apache-2.0, so it can ship. This is the one ready laptop-profile component in the field and the first laptop-only method with calibrated confidence; compare its review load with the 2B's 78.8 checks per 100 pages. Profile 1.

4. Few-label adaptation learning curve on Lincoln and Stanton at 0, 25, 50, 100, 200 labelled gaps: threshold and prior refit; doc-split-v1 CRF and calibration refit; few-shot examples in the window prompt; LoRA continuation of the 2B (one epoch over the labelled windows plus a replay of corpus windows). Open models free on FarmShare or Sherlock; the Gemini arm about $1 per box per setting at list, so the prompt arm fits under $3 only at two or three settings or in batch. Decides what the review screen's corrections are worth and what the kit should promise. Profiles 1, 4, 5.

5. TABME++ test as the public anchor. Three arms. (a) The students and the 27B teacher over the 6,236-decision test set on FarmShare or Sherlock: free; compare with A05 Table 4 (fine-tuned 7B 0.987 page F1, encoders 0.78-0.83). (b) Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, text only, adjacent pairs and K=7, the configuration A04 costed at $2.79 and $1.09 for the whole test set: under $3, directly comparable with A04 Table 4 (0.883 and 0.863). (c) Our image-plus-text Gemini 3.8 Flash window on a 500-page subset: about $1.80. Together they tell us whether our students' 79% on the test boxes is the material or the model, and whether image input helps on clean scans where A04 says it does not. Profiles 1, 3, 4. Mind the UCSF terms on the page images before any of this ships in a card.

6. Window size and modality on the development boxes: K in {3, 5, 7, 10, whole folder}, text-only against image-plus-text, with the 2B (free, Mac or cluster) and Gemini 3.8 Flash. The Gemini sweep over 551 pages at four new settings is about $8 at list, $4 in batch, so it exceeds $3 and needs the owner, or a 150-page subset. Tests A04's two claims on photographed material. Profiles 1-3.

7. Self-ensembling for the open models: majority vote over three window offsets and two prompts for the 2B, 4B and teacher on the test boxes and Lincoln. Free on FarmShare or Sherlock. A26's vote bought 1.3 points over its best model and the two cloud models agreed on every Dean gap, so the expected gain is small; worth one run because it is free and it is the only lift available to profile 1 without labels.

8. OpenPSS-long and -short with the students and, on a subset, Gemini windows. Students free on the cluster (the Parquet is 2.56 GB); Gemini on 1,000 pages about $3.61 at list, $1.81 in batch. 9. A known-document-count prior on Lincoln. The catalogue gives 72 items for the 261 canvases; constrain the segmentation to about that many documents (keep the 72 most confident boundaries, or cluster to that count as A12 does) and score. Free, Mac, cached answers. Speculative; A12 says the count "helps considerably" but could not be read. It is the only label-free signal a finding aid offers.

Paid routes are needed only for items 1, 4 (prompt arm), 5b-c, 6 and 8, each scoped under $3 except item 6 in full. Everything else is free on the Mac or the clusters.

## Open questions

1. HR-DocStream. Not found in arXiv, Hugging Face, Semantic Scholar or the web under that name or a paraphrase, in the screen's three searches or this read's two. The owner should say where the name came from; until then it is not a benchmark we can run.

2. Does the cloud window hold on Lincoln? Unmeasured; test 1. The whole strand's recommendation for profiles 1 and 5 depends on it.

3. What is in doc-split-v1? Its training data, base models, size and speed are undisclosed. Its zero-shot drop to 0.704 on TABME++ suggests it was trained on synthetic business streams of its own. Test 3 measures it; the card will not tell us.

4. How many corrections buy a point? No published learning curve exists; test 4 is ours to run.

5. Is text-only enough on photographs? A04 says yes on clean OCR; our cues are partly visual; test 6.

6. The metric for the owner. Pages in an exact document is right for the harm a user feels; PQ, MNDD and STP are what the field publishes and what a card for other users will be compared on. Test 2 computes all of them; the owner decides which the app shows.

7. Licences for a shipped kit. TABME++'s MIT covers the packaging; the Truth Tobacco Industry Documents' own terms were not read. OpenPSS has no licence of its own. DocSplit is non-commercial. Any card the Processor ships that quotes a public figure should name the terms.

8. Two items could not be read: the clustering paper (A12) and the TF-IDF-versus-embeddings preprint (A14). Both are label-free or cheap-baseline angles that bear on profile 1. A later session with a browser could read them; neither changes the ranking above.
