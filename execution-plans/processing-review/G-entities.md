## Question

The app writes 2-6 subject tags and a date per document and no entity tags (`SPEC/tag-format.md`), but its automatic tagging prompt already asks the model for an author, a recipient and their locations and writes them into the segment JSON sidecar (`OCR/SegmentJSONBuilder.swift`), where nothing reads them: the Reader has no consumer (no reference to these fields in its sources). The strand asks four things. Whether sender, recipient, salutation and signature extraction would improve the two decisions that matter now, the enclosure relation (B-dating: only 11 of 131 undated documents follow a document with a cue word, so a relation detector needs other evidence) and the document boundary (A-segmentation: the Lincoln failure is 32-44 false splits inside long documents, with every true boundary found). How robust entity extraction is at our OCR error levels, which run from about 4% character distance on typescript read by the frontier models and the 27B to about 15% on handwriting and about 19% on Apple Vision's text. Whether small local extractors (GLiNER and its successors, Apple's tagger) can replace the LLM per route. Whether entity linking with time ranges is a traceable dating cue, what minimal metadata output would be worth offering and in what format, and what test set it needs.

## What the field does now

### Letter metadata extraction

Two sources do exactly our optional feature. Strutz, Scholger and Vogeler (G16, DHd 2026) run an LLM pipeline over the Hammer-Purgstall correspondence (3,545 transcribed letters, 1794-1856, 69% German) to fill CMIF: writer, place of writing, date and language, then reconcile writer to the GND and place to GeoNames, with a second LLM round for ambiguous candidates (slides 16, 20). On a 100-letter sample (82% with a dateline, 89% signed; slide 17) the writer F1 after GND enrichment is 93.6% for a local Qwen3-14B at Q6 (7 false positives), 99.5% for GPT-5.2 (1) and 99.5% for Claude Sonnet 4.6 (0); place after GeoNames enrichment 85.7% (18 FP), 89.5% (2) and 92.0% (2); date normalisation 86.0%, 88.6% and 88.7%; language 99.5-100% (slide 27). The gap between local and commercial is in the preparation stage: Qwen's writer field reached a unique GND match on 27% of letters and left 47% ambiguous, Claude 66% and 19% (slides 28, 30). Qwen produced 90% of its false positives when only a surname was available; all models failed when the place was in the body rather than the dateline (slide 32). Cost for the 100 letters: Claude $1.54 extraction plus $0.43 disambiguation (418,775 tokens), GPT-5.2 $0.59 plus $0.13; Qwen ran on an RTX 4500 (slide 26). The recipient was not extracted at all, because 74% of the letters are to Hammer-Purgstall and the edition knows the rest. No code or prompt is published; the slides are CC BY 4.0.

The RISE-UNIBAS humanities benchmark (G17) has a Business Letters task: 68 German business letters of a Basel shipping company, 1926-1935, 57 with ground truth, 98 scans of 2479 by 3508 pixels, 33 single-page, 32 signed and 25 unsigned; fields send date (ISO 8601), sender persons and receiver persons as "Surname, Given name"; zero-shot from all pages of a letter in one request at temperature 0; exact set matching, with a 24-person list of alternate spellings the model never sees; F1 macro over the three categories is the ranking metric. Persons the annotators inferred from a role ("der Präsident" at that date) or from the correspondence history are marked `<Name>` and `<<Name>>` and excluded from scoring, so naming them counts as a false positive. Across 96 model configurations the median F1 macro is 0.55 (IQR 0.51-0.59), 0.53 on unsigned and 0.59 on signed letters (card, Results). Issue 117 (G18) proposes a separate benchmark that sends all 57 letters in one request and scores named, role-inferred and correspondence-inferred persons separately. Code GPL-3, ground truth and prompts CC BY 4.0, scans Public Domain Mark 1.0.

Nothing else was found on sender or recipient extraction from letters. The recipient-line paper (G19, DAS 2022) could not be read. The email-zoning literature (Okapi: 625 emails in Portuguese, Spanish and French; earlier Jangada and Zebra) detects salutation, closing and signature zones in emails, which is the nearest structural model to a letter's opener and closer; the TEI "Letter Openers and Closers" chapter gives the schema (`<opener>` and `<closer>` holding `<salute>`, `<signed>`, `<dateline>`) and notes that early modern letters follow a fixed sequence while modern ones use "Dear Friend" and "Yours most faithfully" (its examples are German letters 1605-1858).

### Named entity recognition on historical text, and OCR noise

Supervised encoders remain the top of the field. The survey (G04) reports HIPE-2020 strict F1 of 84 (French), 79 (German) and 63 (English) for the best system, in-domain pretraining on historical OCR as "most of the time beneficial", and OCR noise and historical spelling as "still the main sources of errors" (Sections 6, 7). LLM prompting trails: DeepSeek-V3 on HIPE-2022 reaches strict micro F1 0.434 zero-shot and 0.561 with one retrieved example against supervised results such as 0.934 on ajmc German (G11, Tables 2, 5: "prompting cannot yet replace supervised fine-tuning for high-accuracy historical NER"); four open Instruct models "encounter multiple difficulties handling the noisy input documents, scoring lower than fine-tuned dedicated NER systems, yet the resulting predictions provide entities that can be used in further tagging processes by human annotators" (G10, abstract). The IJDH 2025 study of a 1684-1732 ego-document finds the opposite on clean edited text: LLMs "significantly outperform" a traditional NER baseline on complex person references, and project annotations across a bilingual edition at over 97% (abstract). The difference is the input: clean transcription against OCR.

The noise curve is measured by Hamdi et al. (G09). A BiLSTM-CNN-CRF tagger on English CoNLL-03 scores 90.90 F1 on clean text, 87.45 after re-OCR of the clean image (CER 1.7%, WER 8.5%), 74.11 under character degradation (CER 3.6%, WER 21.8%), 71.03 under blur (CER 6.3%, WER 20.0%), 70.82 under the mixed degradation (CER 6.9%, WER 22.8%) and 60.31 under heavy blur (CER 41.3%, WER 54.0%) (Tables 1, 2). The authors' band: "all the systems achieve satisfying results when the OCR error rates are between 1% and 5% at the character level and 8% and 15% at the word level" (Section 4), linking holds to CER 7% and WER 17%, and 81.49% of the damaged entities are within edit distance 2 of their true form; deletions are survived, substitutions and insertions are not, and a wrong first-character case is a specific NER failure (Sections 4, 5). The survey adds van Strien et al.'s drop for persons from 87% to 63% between good and poor OCR, HIPE-2020's finding that entity-level noise of 0.1 "may halve" performance, and Huynh et al.'s rule that post-correction helps NER above 3% CER and 20% WER and hurts below (G04 Sections 4.2.1, 6) [M in the survey, secondary]. Two 2025-26 papers show the remedy that fits a training kit: a German BERT fine-tuned with synthetic OCR errors injected into clean training text reaches the best F1 (77.9%) on uncorrected Tesseract output of historical vocational documents, above the same model trained on clean or on noisy text alone (arXiv 2601.00488, Table 4); FinArcNER, trained with noisy Finnish archival text, holds F1 0.920 on modern and 0.871 on archival text (abstract).

Zero-shot span taggers. GLiNER (G35) is a DeBERTa-v3 encoder that scores candidate spans against label embeddings, trained on Pile-NER (44,889 passages, about 13k entity types); the large model (0.3B) averages F1 60.9 over seven out-of-domain benchmarks against 47.5 for ChatGPT and 53.7 for UniNER-7B, the small (50M) 52.7 and the medium (90M) 55.4 (Table 1); over 20 datasets 47.8 against 36.5 (Table 2); the multilingual model averages 32.9 on MultiCoNER against 29.9 for ChatGPT and 54.9 for supervised XLM-R (Table 3). The paper notes that it "underperforms compared to UniNER on tweet-based NER datasets" and names noisy data as the open weakness (Section 4.1). GLiNER2 (205M) adds a schema interface (entities with descriptions, classification, and parent-child structures such as `field::type::description`) in one forward pass and matches GPT-4o on CrossNER (0.590 against 0.599, Table 3), but its structured extraction "was not evaluated due to the absence of established zero-shot benchmarks" (paper). GLiNER2.5 (release note, 2026) replaces span enumeration with boundary prediction, takes 4,096-word inputs, and ships small (74M), base (194M) and multilingual (287M) weights; on Few-NERD the base scores 55.14 against GLiNER2's 47.22, on CrossNER 58.30 against 59.06. All GLiNER-family weights checked are Apache-2.0 (Hugging Face API, this read). No evaluation of any GLiNER on OCR text or historical material was found (two searches).

### Key information extraction from archival records

Two staged-versus-one-shot measurements on archival images. Vafaie et al. (G25, CIKM 2025) annotate 19 fields on German compensation index cards (BZKOpen) and compare, on 78 test cards: a fine-tuned Donut at 59% exact match (fine-tuned on 361 cards; the base 56%), the rule-based OCR baseline at 74% on three layouts, GPT-4o-mini zero-shot 72% and five-shot 85%, and InternVL2.5-38B zero-shot 83% exact and 91% within three edits (NED 0.080), which fine-tuning on the same 361 cards lowers to 79% (Tables 1-3). Names reach 85% exact zero-shot (Table 4). The authors conclude that "fine-tuning alone on a rather small dataset cannot close the performance gap" and that larger models (76B, 78B) do worse than the 38B (Section 6). Greif et al. (G13) on 1754-1870 German city directories find that NER from post-corrected OCR text beats NER from the page image: Gemini 2.0 Flash strict 74.92 from OCR text against 68.76 from the PNG, fuzzy 88.61 against 80.86, and 77.48 / 89.32 from ground-truth text; by field, surnames 97.70 fuzzy from either text, 89.37 from the image (Tables 3, 4); "separating out the steps and including mLLM-based OCR Post-Correction yields the highest accuracy" (Section 1). The input CER was 3.67% for the OCR text and 0.84% after post-correction (Table 2), so the text-route loss from 3.7% CER was 2.6 strict points and 0.7 fuzzy points for Gemini and 4.7 / 3.4 for GPT-4o.

### Entities and time

The literature uses dates to help linking, not entities to find dates. DELICATE (G42) re-ranks Wikidata candidates for historical Italian with a time feature, the gap between the document date and the entity's earliest Wikidata date; the feature ranks fifth in permutation importance (about 0.05) behind vector distance and string similarity, there is no ablation that removes it, and the rule-based temporal filter of the comparison system C-BLINKITA scores below plain BLINK (38.41 against 50.24 on one set), which the authors blame on temporally irrelevant dates such as Italy's inception (Figure 4, Table 6). HIPE-2026 (G14) asks whether a person was at a place near a newspaper's publication date; two of seventeen teams applied Wikidata birth and death dates as consistency rules, without an isolated gain; the best run reached macro recall 0.748 against a zero-shot Ministral baseline of 0.582, and the DS@GT system found that "minimum character distance alone captures most of the classification signal" and that pair-level splits inflate scores by 25-37 points "because entity mentions recur across documents" (G14 Table 3; arXiv 2607.19718 abstract). Dalli and Wilks (G38) is the entity-as-time-proxy source the screen found, and it is a remark, not a method (above). No study was found that dates a document from the time ranges of the entities it names.

### Output format

CMIF (G20) is the interchange format correspondence projects publish: a TEI header with `correspDesc` per letter, each with `correspAction type="sent"` and `type="received"` holding `persName` (mandatory; the string "Unknown" when unknown), optional `orgName` and `placeName`, and `date` with `@when`, `@from`/`@to` or `@notBefore`/`@notAfter` in YYYY-MM-DD, YYYY-MM or YYYY; `@cert` ("low") marks an uncertain attribution and `@evidence` ("conjecture") one the editor inferred rather than read; `persName/@ref` to GND, VIAF, LC, BNF or NDL and `placeName/@ref` to GeoNames are "strongly recommended, if available"; the file itself must carry a CC-BY 4.0 or CC0 statement (documentation-en.md). The spec is BSD-2 and CC BY 4.0, version 1.1 adds a Schematron. G16's slides 5-6 show exactly this encoding for one letter.

## Comparison with what we measured

What we already have, unmeasured for quality. Every automatic tagging run has been asking for the author, recipient and their locations and writing them into the sidecar; the bench cached these answers for 300 documents from Gemini and three open models. Gemini names an author on 59% of all documents and on 70% of letters and memos, a recipient on 83% of letters and memos; the open models agree with it on the author's surname 75-90% of the time where both answer, and disagree mostly by answering null (67-85 one-sided nulls on author against 128-133 joint answers). There is no truth, so none of this is accuracy; but the shape matches G16 (local model behind, commercial near-complete on the writer) and G17 (recipients harder than senders where unsigned or role-addressed letters are common).

Traceability, which the owner's dating rule demands, is the first finding to carry forward. Of 177 author names Gemini gave, 144 (81%) are in the document's own OCR text once letter-spacing artefacts are tolerated, 16 (9%) appear only in the neighbour-context section of the prompt, and 17 (10%) appear nowhere in the prompt. The nowhere cases are recognisable: "Herbert Hoover" and "Ray Lyman Wilbur" in a Stanford collection, "A. Lawrence Lowell" in the Lowell Papers, "Irving Kristol and Bea Kristol" in the Kristol Papers, "Jimmy Carter" in the McCloskey letters. Some are the model completing a garbled or abbreviated signature from knowledge of the collection, some may be right, none can be checked against the page. The 16 borrowed from the context are the enclosure case in miniature: the cover's author attributed to the enclosure, which is sometimes what the archivist wants and sometimes wrong. G13 measured the same thing from the other side (NER from the image hallucinates more than NER from text: 68.76 against 74.92 strict) and G16 listed hallucinated names as the risk of the method (slide 14). The consequence is the same as B-dating's: an entity the app shows must carry its quote or its location, and a scorer must check the quote, with spacing-tolerant matching because ABBYY spaces letters.

What the enclosure question gets from entities. B-dating asked whether the cover naming the enclosure's author or subject would detect the relation that cue words miss. Measured as bare name overlap it does not: the current document's extracted author or recipient surname appears in the previous document's text in 50% of the strict enclosure-consistent cases and in 43% of the cases with no dated same-year document within three positions. Folders are about the same people, so names recur regardless of relation, which is the effect HIPE-2026's lightweight team measured as a 25-37 point inflation from entity recurrence across documents. The strict class is also mostly clippings, reports and notes (7 + 3 + 3 of 33) rather than letters, so the enclosure's own form, which B-dating lists as signal (b), is a better cue than its names. What remains possible is a relation classifier that sees both texts and returns a quoted cue, where names are one feature among the cue words, the form and the adjacency; it should be measured, not assumed.

What segmentation gets. Openings and closings are a strong cue where they fire and a rare one. Over 35,563 cleaned gaps, a salutation at the top of the right page predicts a boundary at 92% precision but appears on 16% of boundaries; a closing at the foot of the left page likewise at 91% and 13%; together 90% at 24%, and both at once 98.7% at 4.6%. The false fires are running headers (a memo repeating "To: M. M. May" with a page number on every page) and are removable by a rule. Absence of the cue is nearly uninformative: the boundary rate without either cue is about 22% against a base rate of 27%. On Lincoln, through Apple Vision's text of 1860s hands (mean 624 characters a page), the salutation fired on 8 of 79 boundaries and the closing on 2, so the cue cannot by itself undo the 32-44 false splits; its value there depends on the HTR quality, and the letters do carry the forms ("Sir:", "Your obedient servant"). This matches the field's view: the cloud windows already read these forms (A-segmentation: the prompt's cues are salutations, memo headings, bylines), and what a rule adds is a cheap, checkable vote for the laptop profile and a precision guard for the review screen.

Robustness to our OCR. Our NES is one minus the normalised character Levenshtein distance, so one minus NES is a character error figure of the same kind as CER with a different normaliser [I]. The frontier models and the 27B read the 96 truth pages at NES 94-96 (typewritten 95.6 for the hosted Qwen3-VL 8B), handwriting at about 85, and Apple Vision at 81 overall. Against Hamdi's bands that puts typescript read by a good model inside the 1-5% range where a 2016-era tagger kept 87-91 F1, and handwriting or Vision text in the 15-20% range where the same tagger lost 16-30 points (Table 2). Three things soften this. Modern encoders and LLMs degrade more slowly than that tagger (the dslim BERT study in the search; G13's 2.6-point strict loss at 3.7% CER; our open models' 75-90% surname agreement on the same ABBYY text). Entities on a letter are concentrated in the dateline, salutation and signature, which are short and high-contrast, so page-level error rates overstate the error on the fields that matter [I]. And a surname damaged by one or two substitutions is still matchable at edit distance 2 (81.49% of damaged entities, G09), which a scorer and a reconciliation step should allow. A switch-off threshold for the optional feature cannot be read from the literature because no source measures extraction on handwriting at our error levels; Test 2 measures it.

Which public benchmark makes our own less necessary. None replaces it: no public set has English 20th-century letters with sender, recipient, date and place labels, and none has phone photographs. Two are worth borrowing. The G17 Business Letters task is the one external anchor with the same fields and a running scorer; its exact-match F1 with alternate names and its three-way person classes (G18) are the scoring we should adopt, with a fuzzy variant per Ehrmann (strict and overlap) and an edit-distance tolerance per Hamdi. HIPE data is for noise-robustness experiments only, under a non-commercial licence. CMIF is the schema to emit if the feature ships. Our own set is the 300 sampled documents, already answered by four models, plus a stratified top-up of letters without datelines, unsigned, handwritten, which G16 and G17 both found to be where models fail.

## Strand questions

### 1. Sender, recipient, salutation and signature as inputs to enclosure detection and boundary decisions

For the enclosure relation the measured answer is that names alone do not discriminate (50% against 43%), because a folder's documents share their people. Names can still serve inside a relation classifier as one feature with the cue words (present in one strict case in six, B-dating), the candidate's form (clipping, report, memo heading with no dateline), the cover's closing on its last page and the candidate's opening on its first, and adjacency. The classifier's reply must quote the cue it used, and the quote is what the scorer checks. The already-extracted `author_name` and `recipient_name` are the cheapest version of the name feature and cost nothing. The dating consequence stays as B-dating set it: `Sent With` takes the cover's own date, and the relation's confidence, not the date's, is what is thresholded.

For boundaries the measured answer is a high-precision, low-recall rule: either cue at 90% precision on 24% of boundaries, both at 98.7% on 4.6%. Three uses follow. As a feature in the feature model and the window combiner, where it will mostly agree with the cloud models and help the 2B student, whose confidence does not rank its errors (A-segmentation). As a precision guard in the review screen: a gap with a salutation on the right and a closing on the left needs no review. As a running-header rule: a repeated "To:" line with a page number is a continuation signature, not a start, which is the mirror of the false-fire cases. It does not fix Lincoln, where the OCR does not carry the cue; there the signal would have to come from the HTR route the OCR strand recommends for manuscripts, after which the same rule applies.

### 2. Robustness to our OCR error levels

Stated as bands with our figures. Typescript read by Gemini, Claude or the Qwen 27B (character distance about 4-5%): the taggers of the literature keep 87-91 F1 here and LLM extraction loses 1-3 points against clean text (G13); the feature can run. Handwriting read by the same models (about 15%) and anything read by Apple Vision (about 19%): the literature's taggers lose 16-30 points, linking fails, and no source measures LLM extraction here; the feature should be off by default until Test 2 says otherwise, and extracted names should be confirmed by a spacing-tolerant, edit-distance-2 match against the text before they are shown. Post-correction before extraction pays only above about 3% CER (Huynh in G04; G13's 3.67% to 0.84% then NER gain), which is the handwriting and Vision band, not the typescript band. The kit remedy is noise-aware fine-tuning of a small tagger on the user's own corrected text with Hamdi's four degradations injected, which two 2025-26 papers found to be the best of three training regimes; it is a profile 4 and 5 route.

### 3. GLiNER and similar local models against LLM extraction, per route

R1. Apple's `NLTagger` gives person, place and organisation spans on device in English and seven other languages at no cost; nothing measures it on OCR text, and it returns spans, not roles, so sender and recipient need a rule (the signature block's name is the sender, the salutation's or inside-address name the recipient). Foundation Models' guided generation can return a typed record on an Apple Intelligence Mac with undocumented limits. Both are tests, not results.

R2. GLiNER2.5 small (74M), base (194M) and multi (287M) are the laptop extractors: Apache-2.0, CPU, a schema that returns sender, recipient and place as fields rather than spans, 4,096-word inputs. Their published figures are on modern benchmarks (CrossNER 58.30, Few-NERD 55.14 for the base) and the structured-extraction mode is unevaluated by its authors; on OCR text GLiNER's own paper predicts weakness. The Qwen 2B and 4B students and the 27B already answer the tagging prompt and agree with Gemini on 75-90% of author surnames; they cost 1-20 seconds a document on a Mac (date-model.md, ocr-mac-local.md). The question for profile 1 is therefore whether a 74-287M extractor at a fraction of a second matches a 2-4B VLM at seconds, on our text; Test 1 answers it on the same 300 documents.

R3 and R4. The entity fields already come with the tagging call at $4.07 per 1,000 documents text-only (measured); a separate pass would roughly double that and is worth it only if Test 5 shows the joint prompt costs accuracy. G16 puts Claude Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.2 at 99.5% writer F1 after reconciliation on clean transcriptions, and Gemini 3.8 Flash is the app's default; the few-shot prompts with normalisation rules that G16 used cost about 4,200 tokens a letter, which is where its $15-20 per 1,000 letters came from.

R5. The Claude plan through `claude -p` runs the same prompt at no money, and G16's top performer is the Claude family, so this is the natural free arm for profile 3.

R6. None found: Azure's prebuilt models cover invoices, receipts, contracts, tax and identity documents and a custom extractor needs labelled samples; the search returned nothing for Document AI or Textract on letters, and Transkribus was not searched for entities.

R7. Bulk runs of the open models and the fine-tunes: a noise-aware GLiNER2.5 or hmBERT tagger on the user's corpus, minutes on one L40S [I].

R8. The cascade that fits the traceability rule: a local extractor proposes, a substring check against the OCR text (spacing-tolerant, edit distance 2) confirms or discards, and only documents with a letter form and no confirmed sender or recipient go to the paid route; the confirmed quote is the evidence shown.

### 4. Entity linking with time ranges as a traceable dating cue

The literature runs the other way: DELICATE and two HIPE-2026 teams use the document's date to constrain which entity a mention is, and none measures the gain of the time feature in isolation; the one rule-based temporal filter measured (C-BLINKITA) lowered accuracy. Nothing measured supports dating a document from the office terms or lifespans of the people it names. The idea is nonetheless sound in a narrow form on our corpus, where letters address university presidents, deans and public officials by office: a mention of a person with an office gives a `notBefore` and `notAfter` from Wikidata's position-held qualifiers, and the evidence is checkable (the mention, quoted, plus the Wikidata statement). Three cautions. The G16 reconciliation stage shows how much ambiguity a local model leaves (47% of writers ambiguous before the second round), and a wrong link gives a confident wrong range. Private persons, who are most of a box, have no Wikidata entry; the 24 persons of the G17 letters are such people, and the linking literature's NIL rates (28.67% on MHERCL-ITA, G42) are for public figures in newspapers. And the names Gemini produced from nowhere in the prompt show that the models already apply this knowledge silently; making it explicit is the traceable version of what is happening anyway. It is a bounded free test (Test 7), not a feature.

### 5. A minimal metadata output, its format, and the test set

What to offer. Four fields per document, each with a quote and a confidence: sender (person or organisation), recipient, place of sending, and the document's own date, which the app already has; recipient's place and mentioned persons are later options. This is exactly CMIF's `correspAction sent` and `received`, and the sidecar already carries the first three as `author_name`, `recipient_name`, `author_location`, `recipient_location`. The change is to add the evidence (the quoted string from the OCR text, or `inferred` with the source: a neighbour, the collection, knowledge), a confidence, and the "Unknown" convention, and to keep them in the sidecar and the page-2 header, never as Finder tags: a name is free text that collides with every facet rule in `SPEC/tag-format.md` and has no place in the tag vocabulary. Export as CMIF (one file per box or collection, `persName` mandatory, `@cert="low"` for low confidence, `@evidence="conjecture"` for anything not quoted, dates in CMIF's three precisions, which map onto the Year, Month and Day facets; a `Sent With` date has no CMIF slot and would stay in the sidecar) for the users who feed correspSearch or a TEI edition; authority IDs optional and off by default, since most names in a private box have none. The Reader's use is search and a sender/recipient filter, which can read the sidecar.

The test set. From the owner's corpus: the 88 letters, memos and telegrams among the 300 sampled documents, which already carry four models' answers, plus a stratified top-up of about 60 to cover handwritten letters, letters without a dateline, unsigned letters and role-addressed ones ("Dear Mr. President"), the strata G16 and G17 found hardest. Labels per document: sender, recipient, sending place, each as a normalised name, its quote in the OCR text (or none), and G18's class (named, role-inferred, correspondence-inferred). Drafting by two model families and adjudication, as the OCR truth was built, with the owner reviewing only disagreements, under the owner-review rule. Scoring: exact set match with alternate spellings (G17), overlap match (Ehrmann), edit-distance-2 tolerance (Hamdi), null handling (a wrong name is worse than a null), and an evidence-validity rate (the quote is a substring of the text). As the public anchor, the G17 Business Letters task run unchanged through its harness with our routes, reported beside the median 0.55.

## Open questions

1. What is the accuracy of the fields the app already extracts? Unknown until Test 1; everything else in this strand is conditional on it.
2. Should a name the model knows but the page does not show ever be written? The 17 author names from nowhere are the case. The owner's dating rule says no without evidence; the product question is whether `inferred` with a source is acceptable for entities as `Date Uncertain` is for years.
3. Does the joint tagging prompt lose accuracy against a dedicated call? Untested by any source; Test 5.
4. At what OCR error level does extraction stop paying, and should the feature be off for Apple Vision text and handwriting by default? Test 2.
5. Do openings and closings help the Lincoln case at all once a better HTR route reads the hands, or do 1860s letters need different forms in the rule? A re-run of the Lincoln cue test on the OCR strand's best manuscript text answers it for free.
6. Is CMIF the right export, or is a CSV with the same four fields enough for the users who have asked for nothing yet? A Phase 4 product question; the sidecar change is the same either way.
7. The G19 recipient-line paper and the Business Letters per-model results (the dashboard is a JavaScript application) could not be read; neither changes the ranking.
