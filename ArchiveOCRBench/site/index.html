Archive OCR bench

Six parts: the W41 OCR panel ( routes reading the same 96 truth pages), segmentation (how well each approach splits a box of photographs into documents), the on-device handwriting readers the Vision OCR lab measured, open-weight models run on Stanford's FarmShare GPUs, the panel's pages ranked by difficulty, with cheap-first cascades simulated from the measured readings, and how well the tagger dates a document. Part 1 · OCR panel · 96 truth pages

OCR price and accuracy

Each point is one route: its NES score against what 1,000 pages cost. NES is character edit similarity to the adjudicated truth, so 96 means about 4% of characters would need fixing. Part 2 · Segmentation · two locked test boxes, 363 pages

Segmentation: splitting a box into documents

A box is photographed page by page, and the app has to say where one document ends and the next begins. Each approach is scored on two test boxes (Stanton and RG 165) held out until its settings were frozen on three development boxes. "No checking" is the share of document pages that land in exactly the right document if nobody checks anything. Each approach also flags the boundaries it is unsure of. Checking those flags costs the owner's time, so the table counts them per 100 pages and says how many pages end up right after checking.

Full manual asks about every boundary and costs nothing; it is the floor. Prices are API list and batch prices per 1,000 pages, from tokens measured over all five boxes. Claude and ChatGPT ran on their subscriptions, so theirs are what the same requests would cost on the API. Gemini 3.8 Flash is at an introductory price until 31 Dec 2026; at the later price its $3.61 becomes $7.22 ($3.61 by batch). GLM-5.3 Flash, GPT-5.6 Luna and Qwen3.5 Flash were the cheap models that survived a one-box screen; all three did worse off that box. Qwen3.5 27B on FarmShare is the open-weight model that cleans the corpus labels a small Mac model will be trained on; it runs on Stanford's GPUs, so it has no price per page and is not on the chart, and its confidences are too even to flag on, so reaching 98% means checking every gap, as Full manual does. Every error count here is small, so one or two errors either way is noise, and a zero on 363 pages is not a promise of zero. The fine-tuned Qwen3.5 4B and 2B are small models trained on the owner's corpus with that teacher's cleaned labels, so they can run on an 8 or 16 GB Mac; these are their FarmShare figures at full precision, and the Mac's own 4-bit and 8-bit figures are in seg-students.md. A different truth set and a different score from part 1, so these numbers do not compare with NES. Missed words are truth words the reader did not produce; added words are words it produced that match no truth word. The pick rule was set before the run: of the readers that pass, take the fewest missed, but where two are within 20 per 1,000, take the smaller and faster. Qwen3.5-9B and LightOnOCR-3-4B are 5 apart, so LightOnOCR-3-4B, which is nearly twice as fast, is the pick (owner confirmed 2026-10-15, Vision OCR commit 168af14). Churro's added words come from one page that looped. Both are also on part 1's charts as Mac routes (◇), read on the panel's truth set and scored by NES like every route: on its handwritten chart LightOnOCR-3-4B scores 89.67 and Qwen3.5-9B 87.92 at 4-bit (90.92 hosted). As read, neither model returns word positions, and the app's alignment places only 65% of their words, so handwritten pages will get a separate text page.

Part 5 · Page difficulty and cascades · the same 96 pages

Accuracy on the pages answered, and what to do with the rest

A page that fails is not inaccurate text: it goes on to another model. So here accuracy on the pages a route answered and its failures are counted apart, with page difficulty in the picture, and cascades send each page to a cheap reader first and on to a stronger one when it fails or looks wrong. Every figure is from the panel's measured readings; no new calls were made. The full report, with every table, is ArchiveOCRBench/difficulty.md.

What flags an answered page that is wrong

A cascade sees a failure but not a poor reading. AUC is how well a signal ranks a reader's poor readings above its good ones: 0.5 is chance, 1 is perfect. Length compares the reader's word count with Apple Vision's (free, on the device); disagreement compares its text with a second cheap reader's; kind is the reader's own handwritten-or-mixed label; Vision is whether Vision OCR's page sorter calls the page handwriting, the same for every reader. Part 6 · Dating documents · the owner's own tags as the answer

The year and month the tagger gives a document

Against the owner's tags

What is scoredDocumentsRightShare

Segmentation only proposes boundaries; the tagging call dates each document afterwards, with the prompt TagGenerator.generateTags sends (year always, month only if the document states it, date_uncertain when the year is not in the document itself). Documents are runs of pages with one identity tag set, kept only where the label-clean teacher and the tags agree on every gap around them, sampled across collections; the corpus copies of the test and development boxes are left out. The text sent is ABBYY's layer, not the app's own OCR. A month the reply gives where the owner tagged none is not always invented: most of those months are named in the text, so the owner left them untagged. Full tables: ArchiveProcessor/segbench-results/date-tagging.md.

Part 7 · Public truth sets · not the owner's archive

Reference routes on the public strata

By stratum

StratumPagesRouteAnsweredNESNES, answeredSecond scoreCost

Pages drawn with a fixed seed from published transcriptions (no collection more than a quarter of its stratum), checked against Apple Vision's reading for wrong truth, and frozen before any model read them; the manifest is ArchiveProcessor/segbench-results/bench-corpus/manifest.jsonl. Same prompt, image size and scorer as the 96. Each source keeps its own transcription conventions, so a stratum's score is comparable across routes, not with the 96 or with another stratum. Second score: NES after mapping historical letter forms (long s, ligatures, abbreviation marks) for L5-L8; without diacritics for N1, N3 and N4. Apple Vision runs the app's settings (English, language correction on), so it reads only the Latin-script strata. N4 understates every route twice over: its truth is the main text only, while the prompt asks for everything on the sheet, so a reader that also transcribes the masorah in the margins loses points; and all but one of BiblIA's 132 pages are transcribed without vowel points, which Gemini writes, so for N4 the second score, without marks, is the fairer figure. Gemini's failures were provider errors (HTTP 500 mid-reply, not billed); Qwen's were replies that ran to the 8,192-token limit, mostly repeating a line. Scores only: the page images are not published, whatever their licence allows.

Open line recognisers for the hardest strata (W43.script-specialists), read on our Mac with kraken: its default blla segmenter cuts each page into lines once, and every model reads the same lines, so a model's score includes the segmenter's reading order. That costs a line model against a whole-page reader, which chrF, nearly blind to order, shows. "Unknown" means the model's record gives no training list to check against our sources; a named source means the model was trained on that test source, so its score is an upper bound, not a specialist's. BiblIA's model writes lines in visual order and is reversed to logical order before scoring. Same pages and scorer as the table above; no money. The page-level models (Qari-OCR for Arabic, CHURRO-3B, the Bern Qwen Kurrent models) need FarmShare and are not yet run; the survey is ArchiveProcessor/segbench-results/bench-corpus/specialists.md.

OCR costs. Measured charges for the panel's pinned OpenRouter endpoint (Mistral's published rate for Mistral OCR), as dollars per 1,000 pages, averaged over all 96 pages; the same route cost is used on every chart, so the kind charts compare accuracy at a route's average price. Apple Vision runs on the device and costs nothing, and so do the three models read on our Mac (an M3 Pro with 18 GB): Qwen3-VL 8B (about 41 seconds a page), LightOnOCR-3-4B (about 24) and Qwen3.5-9B (about 38). Qwen3.6 27B on FarmShare is priced at $0 because the cluster bills nothing; what it costs is GPU time, shown in the tables as GPU-hours per 100,000 photos. Claude Sonnet 5.5 ran through the subscription; its price is an estimate from its token counts at $2 and $10 per million input and output tokens, using the standard image size, and was not measured.

Batch prices. Half the measured cost, where OpenRouter lists a batch version of the model at half its standard price (Gemini 3.8 Flash's batch price is half of what the run paid). Mistral OCR's batch price follows Mistral's published 50% batch discount. Routes with no batch point have no batch version in the catalogue.

Limits. One corpus of about 100 pages. Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 3.8 Flash drafted the truth before it was adjudicated, so it may lean toward those families. The "local panel" was chosen for models that could run on a Mac, and ran hosted; two of them have been measured on the Mac itself: Qwen3-VL 8B lost 2.15 points, nearly all of them on four pages it failed to finish, and Qwen3.5-9B gained 2.48, because the hosted route failed four pages and the Mac one; on handwritten pages the Mac's Qwen3.5-9B is 3.0 points below its hosted run. LightOnOCR-3-4B has no hosted route to compare. The FarmShare route is the same model and precision as the hosted Qwen3.6 27B and differs by about a point, which is within noise. Differences of a point or two are not a ranking, and the handwritten chart rests on about 2,200 words.
